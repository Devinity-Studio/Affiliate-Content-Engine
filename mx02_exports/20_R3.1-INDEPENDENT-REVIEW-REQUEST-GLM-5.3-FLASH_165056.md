## 1. Purpose and Intent

Dev8Studio systems are designed to evolve. In practice, two failure patterns repeatedly threaten that evolution:

- **Fossilization** — an Approved Baseline is read as if it forbade replacement, so obsolete components are kept alive far beyond their usefulness, accumulating risk, cost, and maintenance debt.
- **Silent replacement** — components are swapped without assessment, validation, or recorded approval, so continuity guarantees break and historical accountability is lost.

This principle exists to prevent both. It establishes that:

1. The things that must survive change are the system's continuity, contracts, data integrity, and traceability — not the components that happen to implement them today.
2. Replacement is a first-class, governed operation at every scale — from a single component to a complete system or baseline — not an exception to be argued case by case.
3. Historical evidence of previous baselines is retained as evidence, in accordance with applicable record-retention policies, which is different from retaining obsolete implementations in the running system.

The intent is practical, not philosophical: make it easy to justify doing the right replacement, and hard to do it badly.

## 2. Principle Statement

**Core principle:**

> Preserve the system's continuity, contracts, data integrity, and traceability — not the permanence of its current components.

**Supporting statements:**

> An Approved Baseline is a controlled state at a point in time, not a permanent constraint on future evolution.

> **The system must remain accountable, not immutable.**

What this permits: future changes may include component replacement, subsystem replacement, architecture evolution, and complete system or baseline replacement, using any of the Evolution Strategies in §6.

What this requires: every such change must follow the lifecycle in §9 — impact assessment and **validation planning** before approval; **approval** and **execution authorization** under the applicable governance framework; **implementation validation** after execution authorization — proportionate to the scale of the change (§6). For the avoidance of doubt: the validation referenced before approval is *planning* (criteria, method, transition design), and the validation referenced after authorization is *implementation validation* (actual testing and evidence). The two must not be read as the same step, and implementation validation is not required before approval.

**How to read the statement.** It protects four things and refuses to protect one:

| Protected (must survive every change) | Not protected (may be replaced at any scale) |
|---|---|
| Continuity of purpose and behavior | Current components |
| Interface contracts (or their explicit, approved re-contracting) | Current implementation choices |
| Data integrity and the meaning of historical data | Current subsystem composition |
| Traceability — who decided what, on which evidence | The current baseline's status as "latest" |

## 3. Rationale and Design Philosophy

**A baseline is a photograph, not a cage.** An Approved Baseline records that a specific state existed, was verified, and was approved at a point in time. It is evidence. It was never a promise that this state would be optimal forever, and treating it as such converts a governance instrument into an obstacle.

**Continuity is the asset; components are the mechanism.** Components exist to serve continuity. When a better mechanism exists, keeping the old mechanism can itself endanger continuity — through unfixable defects, security exposure, maintenance cost, or the growing impossibility of finding competent maintainers. In that situation, loyalty to the component is disloyalty to the system.

**Evidence is not machinery.** The design documents, test results, and decision records of a previous baseline remain valuable as evidence under applicable retention policies. The obsolete implementation itself does not. Confusing the two produces systems that carry their own history as dead weight in production instead of as retrievable records in history.

**Replacement is normal; irresponsibility is not.** This principle does not make replacement easy in the sense of "uncontrolled." It makes replacement legitimate — provided the change passes impact assessment, validation planning, approval, and execution authorization, and provided the continuity obligations of §§7–8 are met.

**Failure modes this principle is designed to prevent:**

- **Fossilization** — obsolete implementations retained because "the baseline says so."
- **Silent replacement** — components swapped without trace, breaking accountability and reviewability.
- **Continuity loss** — new components that discard data, contracts, or behavioral guarantees nobody remembered to check.
- **Authority erosion** — technical actors treating their own judgment as approval, or treating one approval as covering more than its scope.

## 5. Relationship Between Approved Baseline, Architecture, and Implementation

| Layer | What it is | What a replacement changes | What must be preserved across a replacement |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Approved Baseline** | A controlled, verified state at a point in time, with evidence | May be superseded by a new baseline; retained in history | Its integrity as historical evidence — its content is never rewritten; its lifecycle state changes only by recorded lifecycle events (§11) |
| **Architecture** | The structure: responsibilities, boundaries, and contracts among parts, as of a baseline | May evolve (new structure, new responsibilities) | The continuity obligations: contracts honored or explicitly re-contracted; behavior and data meaning carried forward |
| **Implementation** | The concrete realization of the architecture at that baseline | Freely replaceable — this is the layer replacement acts on | Nothing as such, except insofar as it embodies contracts or holds data; those are carried forward by the transition |

**Key consequences:**

- A baseline freezes a state, not a future. Approving configuration X does not mean configuration X is permanent.
- Architecture is where continuity lives — in contracts and responsibilities. Implementation is where replacement happens.
- A new baseline may carry a new architecture and/or a new implementation. What it may never carry is unrecorded change.

### 5.1 Approved Baseline lifecycle semantics

| Baseline state | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Current Approved Baseline** | The authoritative approved reference for the system at this time. |
| **Proposed / Candidate Configuration** | Under preparation, assessment, or review. No authority until approved under applicable governance. Experiments and prototypes begin here. |
| **Superseded Baseline** | Formerly Current; replaced by a newer approved baseline. Historical evidence; marked superseded-by with date and authority. |
| **Suspended Baseline** | Authority temporarily paused under applicable governance. Suspension is recorded; it is not deletion. |
| **Retired Baseline** | Formally withdrawn from approved-reference status under applicable governance. Its historical record is not erased. |

**Governing rule:**

> **The Current Approved Baseline remains the authoritative approved reference until it is formally superseded, suspended, or retired under applicable governance.**

A proposal, new implementation, or technical experiment does not automatically replace the Current Approved Baseline. Historical baselines must remain identifiable and traceable according to applicable record-retention policies.

## 6. Evolution Strategies and Replacement / Change Levels

### 6A. Evolution Strategies — the strategic choice

| Strategy | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **1. Retain** | Keep the existing component or capability. |
| **2. Extend** | Add capabilities to the existing component. |
| **3. Replace** | Substitute an existing component or subsystem with a different one, as a complete unit. |
| **4. Rebuild** | Construct a new implementation while preserving required continuity. |
| **5. Retire** | Formally discontinue a component or capability under applicable governance. |

These are **alternative strategic choices, not mandatory sequential stages**.

### 6B. Replacement / Change Levels — the scale and impact of a change

The L1–L5 classification describes **how large and how impactful a change is**, assessed on both dimensions: the scope of what is changed *and* the impact it produces. **Classification rule: where a change affects multiple levels, the highest affected impact level governs** — i.e., the change is classified at the level of its greatest impact, not its narrowest scope. A change that touches one component but impacts a subsystem is an L3 change, not an L1 change.

| Level | Scale of change | Continuity obligations (minimum) | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **L1 — Component** | One component, same contracts | Existing interface contract honored exactly; behavior within agreed tolerance | Regression evidence against the existing contract; content identity of new artifact |
| **L2 — Component with contract evolution** | One component; its interface changes version | Explicit version bump; consumers identified; deprecation path documented | Contract tests for both versions; migration notes |
| **L3 — Subsystem** | A set of components and their internal contracts | External contracts preserved or explicitly re-contracted; data mapped through the transition | Interface inventory check; data-migration verification; staged-transition records |
| **L4 — Architecture** | Structural responsibilities, data flows, boundaries | Behavioral continuity at system boundaries or explicit, approved re-contracting; data meaning preserved | Impact assessment; acceptance criteria defined before the change; validation planning and implementation validation results; before/after behavioral comparison |
| **L5 — Complete system / baseline** | The system or the baseline itself | All continuity obligations above, plus: verified data migration, contract mapping, unbroken traceability chain | Full migration verification; ratified acceptance contract; explicit supersession record |

**Standing rules:**

1. **Proportionality:** assessment and validation depth scales with the level — but no level may skip evidence entirely.
2. **Classification responsibility:** the technically responsible actor for the affected system performs the L1–L5 classification. Where classification or approval is **beyond that actor's delegated approval authority**, the matter is escalated to the appropriate authority (including the Owner where the applicable governance framework so provides). Classification is a technical task; authority decisions are governance tasks. The two must not be conflated.

Approval authority and decision rights are determined by the **applicable governance framework and its delegated approval authority** (§10).

## 7. Compatibility and Interface Contracts

Contracts may evolve. APIs, interfaces, data schemas, and behavioral guarantees are not permanently immutable. Controlled versioning, migration, deprecation, and re-contracting are legitimate evolution paths under §§6–10.

**Rules for any replacement:**

1. **Honor, version, or re-contract — never drift.**
2. **Consumers are identified before the switch.** "No one uses this" is a claim that requires evidence.
3. **Adapters and bridges are transition tools, not landmarks.** Each must be recorded with a planned retirement.
4. **Contract verification is evidence**, supported by tests or equivalent verification — not by assertion.

## 8. Data Continuity and Retention

- **Meaning is preserved.** Historical records must remain interpretable under applicable retention policies.
- **Migration is verified, not assumed** — counts, checksums/content identities, sampled meaning-equivalence.
- **Rollback or compensation exists before cutover, proportionate to risk.**
- **No silent data loss or corruption.** This fundamental protection is not weakened by this revision. Data retirement or loss must follow applicable governance and authorization requirements and be recorded as an explicit decision — never as a technical side effect.
- **The audit trail of transformations is retained** under applicable retention policies.
- **Retention is policy-bound, not infinite by default.** Legal, privacy, and security constraints prevail; retention decisions must be explicit and recorded.

## 9. Validation Lifecycle

Validation has two distinct halves. **Validation Planning** (before approval) and **Implementation Validation** (after execution authorization) are different activities. They must not be read as one step.

### 9A. Before approval and execution — Validation Planning (preparation phase)

- **Impact assessment** — what contracts, data, behaviors, and consumers are affected; which Evolution Strategy (§6A) and Change Level (§6B) apply.
- **Affected contract and data inventory.**
- **Risk assessment** — including rollback/compensation feasibility (§8).
- **Proposed acceptance criteria** — defined before approval.
- **Validation planning and transition planning** — how obligations *will be* proven and how the transition *will be* controlled. This is planning only; no implementation validation occurs at this stage.

### 9B. After appropriate approval and execution authorization — Implementation Validation (execution phase)

- **Authorized implementation.**
- **Implementation validation** — actual testing and evidence that applicable obligations and outcomes are satisfied.
- **Controlled transition** — staged cutover proportionate to level.
- **Post-transition verification** — in the real position, before the old capability is retired.
- **Acceptance and baseline recording** — new baseline recorded with its evidence (§11).

### 9C. Prototype and preliminary work

Prototype work or preliminary evidence preparation may occur **where separately permitted** by applicable governance. It is preparation, not authorized implementation: it does not change the Current Approved Baseline and must not be presented as implementation-validation evidence.

### 9D. Validation flexibility

**Validation must prove applicable obligations and outcomes, not necessarily reproduce the old implementation's testing methods.** The new implementation need not use the same tests, technologies, or internal mechanisms. It must provide appropriate, inspectable evidence that applicable requirements, contracts, and continuity obligations are satisfied.

**This section describes concepts. It creates no authority.** Approval powers and decision rights are determined by the applicable governance framework and its delegated approval authority. Decisions outside delegated authority are referred to the appropriate authority.

| Concept | Question it answers | Who holds it | Typical record |

3. No self-authorization. If classification or authority is uncertain: stop the affected activity, preserve evidence, escalate per §6B Rule 2, and wait for explicit authorization.
4. Committing, pushing, deploying, or changing production requires an explicit Owner command, independent of any plan approval.

## 11. Baseline History and Traceability

Every Approved Baseline carries an identity: identifier, date, content identity (hash or equivalent where applicable), and links to its evidence.

- **Immutable Baseline Content.** The approved content of a baseline, once recorded, **is never edited**. Corrections, if any, are made by supersession and lifecycle events — never by rewriting the baseline's recorded content.
- **Append-only Lifecycle Events.** Lifecycle transitions (supersede, suspend, retire, reactivate where permitted) are **new records appended** to the history, each referencing the baseline it applies to, with date and authority. The lifecycle log is append-only; the baseline content it references is immutable. These are two separate things, and neither may be used to alter the other.
- **The traceability chain is unbroken:** previous baseline → impact assessment → Owner decision → approval record → execution authorization → validation planning and implementation-validation evidence → new baseline. Missing links are recorded as missing.
- **Supersession is explicit and bidirectional:** B1 marked superseded-by-B2 (date, authority); B2 names B1 as predecessor.
- **Evidence retention ≠ implementation retention.** Archives preserve historical evidence under applicable retention policies.
- **Multi-version operation is permitted.** The running system may support multiple approved versions or configurations simultaneously — for example, several API versions, or old and new configuration during a migration — **provided each version/configuration in operation is covered by an applicable approval and is recorded and controlled** (which version is current, which are transitional, and when each will be retired). Multi-version operation is a governed state, not a loophole: an unapproved version in production is silent replacement, regardless of how many versions are running.

## 12. Examples and Practical Applications

### 12.1 Required conceptual example — the racing car

An organization builds a racing car. The initial approved configuration uses a **V8 engine**, recorded as **Baseline B1**, the Current Approved Baseline.

Later, the organization develops a **V12 engine**:

1. **Complete-unit replacement is legitimate.** The V12 may replace the V8 as a complete unit. Until the new configuration is approved and recorded, **B1 remains the Current Approved Baseline**; the V12 is a Proposed / Candidate Configuration (§5.1).
2. **Replacement propagates.** Engine mounts, cooling, fuel delivery, gearbox ratios, electronic control mapping, and safety certification may all require modification. Supporting systems are the "contracts" of the car.
3. **Validation planning precedes approval; implementation validation follows authorization.** Bench tests, track tests, and safety checks are planned against criteria defined in advance (§9A), and executed as implementation validation after authorization (§9B). A car that "feels faster" is not an approved car.
4. **Once approved, the new configuration becomes Baseline B2**, explicitly superseding B1. B1's content remains immutable; its lifecycle state changes by a recorded lifecycle event (§11).
5. **The V8 baseline remains preserved** as evidence under applicable retention policies. What does not remain is a V8 bolted into the current race car out of respect for history.

The lesson in one line: **retaining historical evidence does not mean retaining obsolete implementation components. The system must remain accountable, not immutable.**

### 12.2 Software applications (illustrative, non-prescriptive)

- **L1–L2 — Library replacement (Replace).** Swapped wholesale behind a stable interface; regression evidence is the validation. Interface change, if any, is L2: version bump, consumers identified, deprecation path documented. During transition, both versions may run under approval (§11).
- **L3 — Storage subsystem migration (Replace / Rebuild).** Data migrated through defined translation, verified by counts, checksums, sampled meaning-equivalence; rollback tested before cutover.
- **L4–L5 — Whole-system rewrite (Rebuild).** Behavior preserved or explicitly re-contracted; data carried forward by verified migration; new acceptance contract ratified; supersession recorded in both directions.
- **Retire example.** A capability is formally discontinued: consumers migrated, data handled per §8, retirement recorded — not accomplished by quiet deletion.

Pattern in every case: prepare and plan validation (§9A) → approve → (separately) authorize execution → implement and perform implementation validation (§9B) → record baseline → archive old as evidence.
